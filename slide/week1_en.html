CHAPTER 1 • MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM

Skeletal System & Joint Architecture

Structural classification and degrees of freedom in human articulation.

Fibrous Joints

Allow virtually no movement. Connected by fibrous connective tissue.

Example: Sutures of the skull.
Cartilaginous Joints

Allow limited movement. Separated by fibrocartilage discs.

Example: Intervertebral discs, Pubic symphysis.
Synovial Joints

Allow considerable movement. Feature hyaline cartilage & synovial fluid.

Examples: Elbow, Knee, Hip, Shoulder.
Degrees of Freedom in Synovial Articulations
Uniaxial (1 Axis): Operates as a hinge.
e.g., Elbow joint (Flexion/Extension)
Biaxial (2 Axes): Operates in two perpendicular planes.
e.g., Wrist and Ankle joints
Multiaxial (3 Axes): Ball-and-socket configuration.
e.g., Shoulder and Hip joints
CHAPTER 1 • MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM

Skeletal Muscle Architecture

Hierarchical organization from epimysium down to individual sarcomere filaments.

Connective Tissue Sheaths
  • Epimysium: Outer fibrous connective tissue surrounding the entire skeletal muscle belly. Contiguous with tendons.
  • Perimysium: Surround groups of individual muscle fibers (up to 150 fibers) forming a fascicle.
  • Endomysium: Fine connective tissue surrounding each individual muscle fiber/cell.
Microscopic Structure: Sarcomere

The sarcomere is the smallest functional contractile unit of a muscle fiber, bounded from Z-line to Z-line.

  • Myosin (Thick Filament): Contains globular heads with ATPase activity that bind to actin.
  • Actin (Thin Filament): Contains binding sites covered by tropomyosin in the resting state.
  • Troponin: Protein bound to actin; calcium binding causes tropomyosin displacement.
CHAPTER 1 • MUSCULOSKELETAL SYSTEM

The Sliding Filament Theory of Contraction

The 5 sequential phases of muscle force production and filament sliding.

1
Resting Phase

Little free calcium ($Ca^{2+}$) is present in the sarcoplasm. Myosin heads are energized but unable to bind actin.

2
Excitation-Contraction

SR releases $Ca^{2+}$. Calcium binds to troponin, shifting tropomyosin and exposing actin binding sites. Myosin attaches to actin.

3
Contraction Phase

ATP hydrolysis provides energy. The myosin head pulls actin toward the center of the sarcomere (Power Stroke), releasing ADP + $P_i$.

4
Recharge Phase

A new ATP molecule binds to myosin head, releasing it from actin. Myosin re-cocks if $Ca^{2+}$ and ATP remain available.

5
Relaxation Phase

Motor nerve stimulation stops. $Ca^{2+}$ is actively pumped back into SR. Tropomyosin covers actin binding sites again.

Histochemical characteristics and functional capabilities of Type I, IIa, and IIx fibers.

CHAPTER 1 • NEUROMUSCULAR SYSTEM

Neuromuscular Proprioceptors

Sensory receptors providing feedback regarding muscle length and tension.

MS
Muscle Spindles
  • Location: Parallel to intrafusal muscle fibers.
  • Stimulus: Sensations of muscle stretch / length and rate of stretch.
  • Response: Triggers the Stretch Reflex causing rapid reflex contraction of agonist muscle.
  • Application: Exploited in plyometric training (stretch-shortening cycle).
GTO
Golgi Tendon Organs (GTO)
  • Location: In series with extrafusal fibers inside tendons.
  • Stimulus: Increases in muscle tension / force.
  • Response: Triggers Autogenic Inhibition (reflex relaxation of agonist muscle to protect against excessive tension).
  • Application: Neural adaptation in heavy resistance training reduces GTO inhibition.
CHAPTER 1 • NEUROMUSCULAR SYSTEM

Motor Unit Recruitment & Force Graduation

Principles governing motor unit activation and neural force encoding.

All-or-None Principle & Rate Coding
  • All-or-None Principle: All muscle fibers in a single motor unit contract completely when activated by its motor neuron. No partial contraction of a motor unit exists.
  • Rate Coding (Frequency): Varying the firing frequency of action potentials controls force magnitude (Twitch $\rightarrow$ Summation $\rightarrow$ Tetanus).
Henneman's Size Principle
Motor units are recruited sequentially from smallest (Type I) to largest (Type IIx) based on force requirements!

High-threshold Type II fibers are only recruited when heavy loads ($>80\%$ 1RM) or explosive, high-velocity movements are performed.

CHAPTER 1 • CARDIOVASCULAR SYSTEM

Cardiovascular Anatomy & Conduction

Heart structure, systemic blood circulation, and intrinsic electrical signaling.

Cardiac Conduction Pathway
  1. Sinoatrial (SA) Node: Intrinsic pacemaker located in right atrium (~60–100 bpm).
  2. Atrioventricular (AV) Node: Delays impulse slightly to allow ventricular filling.
  3. AV Bundle (Bundle of His): Relays impulse to ventricles.
  4. Purkinje Fibers: Conduct impulse rapidly through ventricular myocardium.
Electrocardiogram (ECG) Deflections
  • P-Wave: Atrial depolarization (contraction).
  • QRS Complex: Ventricular depolarization (and hidden atrial repolarization).
  • T-Wave: Ventricular repolarization (relaxation).
CHAPTER 1 • RESPIRATORY & VASCULAR

Gas Transport & Ventilation Mechanics

Arterial-venous dynamics, pulmonary gas exchange, and blood composition.

Vascular Network
  • Arteries & Arterioles: High-pressure conduits carrying oxygenated blood away from heart. Arterioles regulate blood flow distribution.
  • Capillaries: Single-cell layer vessels facilitating exchange of $O_2$, $CO_2$, nutrients, and metabolic waste.
  • Veins & Venules: Low-pressure return vessels equipped with one-way valves.
Pulmonary Ventilation
  • Inspiration: Contraction of diaphragm & external intercostals expands thoracic cavity, dropping intrapleural pressure below atmospheric.
  • Expiration: Passive relaxation during rest; active internal intercostals & abdominals contract during intense exercise.
  • Hemoglobin: Iron-containing protein in RBCs carrying up to 4 oxygen molecules.
CHAPTER 1 • EXERCISE RESPONSES

Acute Cardiorespiratory Adaptations

Hemodynamic responses to aerobic vs. heavy resistance exercise.

Cardiac Output ($Q$) Formula
\[ Q = \text{Heart Rate (HR)} \times \text{Stroke Volume (SV)} \]
Aerobic Exercise Response
  • Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP): Increases linearly with workload (up to 220 mmHg).
  • Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP): Remains unchanged or decreases slightly due to vasodilation.
  • $VO_2$ Max: Oxygen consumption increases proportionately with intensity.
Resistance Exercise Response
  • Blood Pressure Spikes: Both SBP & DBP increase dramatically (e.g. SBP > 300 mmHg during heavy squat).
  • Valsalva Effect: Expiration against closed glottis increases intra-thoracic pressure and blood pressure spikes.
CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

Skeletal Musculature & Mechanical Advantage

Muscle attachments, moment arms, and mechanical leverage principles.

Muscle Attachments & Terminology
  • Origin: Proximal (towards center of body) attachment; typically stationary.
  • Insertion: Distal (away from center of body) attachment; typically movable.
  • Fleshy vs Tendinous: Muscle fibers attached directly to bone vs connective tissue tendon attachments.
Mechanical Advantage Ratio ($MA$)
\[ MA = \frac{d_m}{d_r} \]

where $d_m$ = moment arm of muscle force, and $d_r$ = moment arm of resistive force.
• $MA > 1.0$: Force Advantage (Requires less muscle force than load).
• $MA < 1.0$: Speed/ROM Advantage (Requires more muscle force than load).

CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

First, Second & Third Class Levers

Anatomical lever configurations in human joint biomechanics.

1st Class Lever: Fulcrum is located between Muscle Force ($F_m$) and Resistance Force ($F_r$).
Examples: Triceps extension at elbow, Neck extension.
Mechanical Advantage: Can be < 1, = 1, or > 1.
MA = Variable (1.0)
CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

Anatomical Planes & Joint Actions

Classifying multi-planar movement patterns in exercise execution.

Sagittal Plane

Divides body into Left & Right sections.

  • Flexion / Extension
  • Plantaflexion / Dorsiflexion
  • Exercises: Squat, Biceps Curl, Running
Frontal Plane

Divides body into Front & Back sections.

  • Abduction / Adduction
  • Lateral Flexion, Inversion/Eversion
  • Exercises: Lateral raise, Jumping Jack
Transverse Plane

Divides body into Upper & Lower sections.

  • Internal / External Rotation
  • Horizontal Abduction / Adduction
  • Exercises: Dumbbell Fly, Medicine ball twist
CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

Human Strength, Power & Torque

Mathematical principles governing force generation and mechanical output.

Definitions & Formulas
  • Force ($F$): \( F = m \cdot a \) (measured in Newtons).
  • Work ($W$): \( W = F \cdot d \) (measured in Joules).
  • Power ($P$): \( P = \frac{W}{t} = F \cdot v \) (measured in Watts).
  • Torque ($\tau$): Rotary force. \( \tau = F \cdot d_\perp \).
Strength vs. Power Distinctions
Strength: Maximal force a muscle group can generate at a specified velocity.
Power: Rate of doing work. Maximal power occurs at intermediate loads (~30–80% 1RM) and velocities!
CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

Biomechanical Factors in Muscle Force

Anatomical and physiological determinants of force production capacity.

Neural & Architectural Factors
  • Cross-Sectional Area (CSA): Force capability is directly related to muscle CSA, NOT muscle volume.
  • Pennation Angle: Angle between muscle fibers and line of pull. Higher pennation = more fibers in parallel = greater maximal force!
Length-Tension & Force-Velocity
  • Length-Tension Relationship: Maximal force is generated at resting length due to optimal actin-myosin overlap.
  • Force-Velocity Relationship: In concentric contractions, force capability decreases as contraction velocity increases.
CHAPTER 2 • RESISTANCE BIOMECHANICS

Sources of Resistance to Muscle Contraction

Characteristics of external resistance modalities in conditioning.

Gravity & Free Weights

Acts strictly vertically. Moment arm changes throughout joint range of motion (ROM), varying torque demand.

Inertia & Friction

Inertia requires extra force to accelerate/decelerate. Friction machines require constant resistive force ($F_f = \mu N$).

Fluid & Elasticity

Fluid resistance increases with movement speed. Elastic bands increase tension proportionally to stretch distance ($F = kx$).

CHAPTER 2 • JOINT BIOMECHANICS

Joint Biomechanics & Injury Prevention

Protecting the lumbar spine, shoulder complex, and knees during heavy resistance training.

Lumbar Spine Safety

Maintain normal lordosis. Utilize the Valsalva Maneuver (expiring against closed glottis) to create intra-abdominal pressure ("fluid ball") to support spine under heavy loads.

Shoulder Complex

Glencohumeral joint is highly mobile but prone to impingement. Require balanced strengthening of rotator cuff muscles (SITS).

Knee Biomechanics

Patella increases lever arm of quadriceps tendon. Avoid knee valgus collapse to reduce ACL strain during heavy lifting.

HIGH-YIELD EXAM PREP • PRACTICE QUESTIONS

NSCA-CSCS Practice Questions (1/2)

Test your knowledge of body systems and biomechanical principles.

Q1: Which of the following muscle fiber types possesses the highest concentration of myosin ATPase and the fastest contraction speed?
A) Type I
B) Type IIa
C) Type IIx
D) Type IC
Correct Answer: C (Type IIx)
Explanation: Type IIx fibers (fast glycolytic) have the highest myosin ATPase activity, allowing the fastest rate of cross-bridge cycling and force production, though they fatigue very quickly.
Q2: Most human limb muscles operate at a mechanical advantage ($MA$) of:
A) Greater than 1.0
B) Equal to 1.0
C) Less than 1.0
D) Infinite
Correct Answer: C (Less than 1.0)
Explanation: Most limbs operate as 3rd class levers ($MA < 1.0$) where the muscle moment arm is shorter than the resistance moment arm. This requires greater internal muscle force than external resistance, but grants high movement velocity and range of motion.
HIGH-YIELD EXAM PREP • PRACTICE QUESTIONS

NSCA-CSCS Practice Questions (2/2)

Test your understanding of neuromuscular proprioception & vascular flow.

Q3: During a heavy squat, sensory feedback regarding rapid muscle stretch is detected by which organ, initiating a reflex contraction?
A) Golgi Tendon Organ
B) Muscle Spindle
C) Pacinian Corpuscle
D) Sarcoplasmic Reticulum
Correct Answer: B (Muscle Spindle)
Explanation: Muscle spindles sense rapid changes in muscle length/stretch and activate the stretch reflex (monosynaptic reflex) to cause agonist contraction.
Q4: Which electrical component on an ECG represents ventricular depolarization?
A) P-Wave
B) QRS Complex
C) T-Wave
D) U-Wave
Correct Answer: B (QRS Complex)
Explanation: The QRS complex represents electrical depolarization of the ventricles. Atrial repolarization occurs simultaneously but is masked by the QRS wave.
